All right, guys, people say "they're only collecting metadata, not the content." Let me show you why that's not as comforting as it sounds. What is metadata? It's data about your data: who you called, when, for how long, from where, and on what device. Stanford researchers asked 823 volunteers to share their phone metadata, covering more than 250,000 calls and 1.2 million texts [1][2]. From that alone, they found names for about 82% of participants and spotted signs of sensitive things like medical conditions, gun ownership and drug use [2].

Here's the definition, what it reveals, real examples and how to limit it.

**Quick answer**

- Metadata explained: the who, when, where and how of your communications.
- Metadata privacy: it can reveal relationships, health and habits [1][2].
- Phone metadata: call logs, text logs, cell tower locations.
- Limit it: choose apps that minimize metadata, turn off unnecessary location.

## The definition

| Communication | Content | Metadata |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Phone call | What you said | Who, when, how long, where |
| Text | The words | Who, when, how often |
| Email | The body | From, to, subject line, time, IP address |
| Photo | The image | Date, time, GPS location, device |

It's a big part of [what you leave behind](/blog/digital-footprint/).

## What does it reveal?

Patterns. A late-night call to a hospital, then a pharmacy, then a specialist tells a story. Calls to a lawyer, a gun store or a clinic tell others. The Stanford team looked at calls to hospitals, pharmacies, religious groups, legal services, firearms retailers and more [2]. Former NSA general counsel Stewart Baker said that "metadata absolutely tells you everything about somebody's life" [2].

## Real examples

1. **Relationships.** Who you contact most, at what hours.
2. **Routines.** Where you sleep, work and spend weekends.
3. **Health.** Repeated contact with clinics or specialists [2].
4. **Beliefs.** Contact with religious or political groups [2].
5. **Photos.** GPS tags in pictures can reveal your home address.

I get it, I get it. You're thinking "I'm not that interesting." Neither were the volunteers. That's the point: ordinary patterns tell a lot.

## How to limit it

This isn't a "trust me" thing. This is "verify me."

1. **Turn off location tagging** in your camera app.
2. **Strip metadata** before sharing photos publicly.
3. **Use messaging apps** that collect less metadata.
4. **Limit location access** for apps that don't need it.
5. **Remember:** end-to-end encryption protects content, not always metadata.
