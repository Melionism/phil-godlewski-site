All right, guys, here's quantitative easing vs tightening without the jargon. Quantitative easing (QE) means the Fed buys large amounts of bonds, adding money to the system and pushing long-term rates down. Quantitative tightening (QT) is the reverse: the Fed lets bonds mature without replacing them, draining money. After 2008 the Fed ran QE1 (about $1.75 trillion), QE2 ($600 billion in Treasuries) and more [1]. Its latest QT ended on December 1, 2025 [2].

Here's what QE does, what QT does, the effects on markets and the debates.

**Quick answer**

- What is quantitative easing: large bond purchases by the Fed [1].
- QE vs QT: adding money vs draining it.
- Quantitative tightening explained: letting bonds roll off the balance sheet.
- Latest QT: ended December 1, 2025 [2].

It's the closest thing to [what "printing money" really means](/blog/fed-creates-money/).

## What does QT do?

The Fed stops replacing maturing bonds. Reserves shrink. Long-term rates can rise. The Fed decided in October 2025 to end its runoff on December 1, 2025 [2].

## Effects on markets

1. **Bond yields:** QE pushes them down; QT can push them up.
2. **Stocks:** cheaper money tends to lift prices.
3. **Housing:** lower mortgage rates during QE [1].

## Debates

I get it, I get it. Critics say QE helped asset owners more than workers and fed inflation later. Supporters say it kept 2008 from becoming another Depression. Both are part of the real debate. This isn't a "trust me" thing. This is "verify me." Read the Fed's own history and decide [1].
