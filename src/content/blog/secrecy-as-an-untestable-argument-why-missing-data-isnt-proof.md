All right, guys, here's a pattern I want you to notice, because it shows up everywhere. "We can't show you the proof because it's classified." "The fact that they won't release it proves it's real." "No evidence just means they buried it." Each of those turns missing evidence into evidence. And that's a trap, because an argument that wins whether the evidence shows up or not can never be tested. Here's a real example of why it matters: before July 2026, a January 2020 intelligence memo on election systems was classified [1]. People assumed it would prove the vote was compromised. Once released, it said systems would be "difficult to manipulate on a wide enough scale to alter the election outcome" [2].

Here's the logic, examples, the burden of proof and what fair skepticism looks like.

**Quick answer**

- Absence of evidence election claims: treating missing evidence as proof.
- Argument from secrecy: "they're hiding it, so it's true."
- Unfalsifiable election claims: claims no evidence could disprove.
- Why secrecy isn't proof: hidden material can support either side.

## The logic

| Statement | Problem |
| --- | --- |
| "It's classified, so it must be damning" | Assumes content |
| "No evidence means coverup" | Can't be disproven |
| "When it's released you'll see" | Delays testing forever |
| "Only X% has been revealed" | Number with no source |

The "25%" line is one example: [the figure repeated online](/blog/only-25-percent-election-evidence-revealed/).

## Examples

1. **The January 2020 memo**: assumed damning, released with a narrower conclusion [1][2].
2. **"The real data"** promised at the 2021 Cyber Symposium, which a reviewer said wasn't 2020 election data [3].
3. **The Kraken suits**: promised evidence, dismissed in court.

## Burden of proof

The person making the claim has to show the evidence. That's how courts work, and it's fair: if I accuse you of something, I need to prove it, not you.

## Fair skepticism

This isn't a "trust me" thing. This is "verify me." Skepticism of government is healthy. Agencies have hidden things before, and some were exposed. Fair skepticism says: "I'll wait and see what the documents show." It doesn't say: "I already know what they show." Watch how promises played out with [what Lindell promised and what followed](/blog/mike-lindell-election-evidence-promises/), and how records become public in [how secret records become public](/blog/classified-information-declassified/).

## FAQ

**Is missing evidence proof of a coverup?** No.

**What's an unfalsifiable claim?** One no evidence could disprove.

**Is government secrecy real?** Yes, sometimes; that's why release and review matter.

**Who carries the burden of proof?** The person making the claim.

**What's fair skepticism?** Waiting for documents before deciding.

## What to do next

1. Ask: "What would change your mind?"
2. Wait for the release.
3. Judge the content, not the secrecy.
