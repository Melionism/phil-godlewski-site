All right, guys, "threat modeling" sounds like something for spies. It's not. Threat modeling for ordinary people is just a short plan: what do I want to protect, from whom, and how much effort is it worth? The Electronic Frontier Foundation's security planning guide boils it down to six questions [1]. Answer them honestly and you'll stop wasting money on gadgets you don't need and start fixing the holes that actually matter.

Here's why it matters. Most real losses come from ordinary attacks. Phishing was the single most common complaint to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center in 2025, with 191,561 reports [2]. The FTC says Americans reported $15.9 billion in fraud losses in 2025, and text messages were the most common way scammers made contact [3].

Here are the questions to ask yourself, the fear of exposure, real vs exaggerated risks, building your own model and how it connects to your data trail.

**Quick answer**

- Personal threat model: a short answer to "what, from whom, how bad, how likely, how much effort."
- Threat model privacy: protect what matters most first.
- How to create a threat model: use the six questions [1].
- Most likely threats: phishing, scams, reused passwords [2][3].

## Questions to ask yourself

The EFF's six questions [1]:

1. What do I want to protect?
2. Who do I want to protect it from?
3. How bad are the consequences if I fail?
4. How likely is it that I will need to protect it?
5. How much trouble am I willing to go through?
6. Who are my allies?

## Real vs exaggerated risks

| Risk | How common | Effort to reduce |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Phishing and scam texts | Very common [2][3] | Low |
| Reused passwords | Very common | Low |
| Account takeover | Common | Low to medium |
| Stalkerware from someone close | Less common, serious | Medium |
| Targeted state hacking | Rare for most people | High |
| "Quantum exposure" | Not a near-term personal risk | — |

Read [real vs exaggerated privacy risks](/blog/real-vs-exaggerated-privacy-risks/).

## Building your own model

Here are three quick examples:

| Person | Protects | From | First steps |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Retiree | Savings, identity | Phone and text scammers | Two-factor login, call-back rule |
| Parent | Kids' photos, location | Strangers online | Private accounts, location off |
| Person leaving an abusive relationship | Location, messages | An ex-partner | New passwords, device check, safety plan |

I need you to notice: none of these starts with buying something. They start with knowing who the threat is.

## How it connects to your data trail

This isn't a "trust me" thing. This is "verify me." Your threat model decides your priorities, and the first priority for almost everyone is the same: lock down your accounts. One leaked password shouldn't unlock your whole life. Read [password manager](/blog/password-manager/).
