All right, guys, if you want to check a decode, you need the original drop. Here's where people find them and why you should be careful with each source. Q's posts are collected by aggregator sites. A research team that compared six of them, qagg, qalerts, operationq, qanon.news, qanon.pub and qmap.pub, found they disagreed a lot: from 30,320 collected drops, they counted 4,961 distinct ones, and settled on 4,949 that at least five sites included [1]. Agreement between sites was poor, and the team found 299 Q-tripcode posts that no aggregator archived at all [1]. In 2021 one count put the total at 4,634 per qmap [2]. So "the archive" isn't one thing. Sites like qalerts.app let you search by text and see the drop number and timestamp, for example drop #34 from November 1, 2017, at 10:56 PM Eastern [3]. The best practice is to read the drop in at least two archives, note the number, date and time, and only then read anyone's decode, including mine.

Here are available archives, finding an original post, testing a new decode, and independent research.

**Quick answer**

- Q drops archive: several aggregator sites, not one official archive [1].
- Disagreement: six sites showed poor agreement; 4,949 drops in the canonical set [1].
- Gaps: 299 tripcode posts never archived [1].
- Method: read the drop in two archives before the decode.
