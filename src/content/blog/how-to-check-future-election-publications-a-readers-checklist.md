So here's a checklist you can use every time, whoever is publishing, including me. It's four steps: source, original text, independent review, and waiting. Here's why it works. And when Mike Lindell's "election data" was handed to an independent reviewer, the reviewer concluded it wasn't 2020 election data at all [2].

**Quick answer**

- How to check election claims checklist: source, original text, independent review, waiting.
- Election misinformation checklist: who said it, what the document says, who checked it, what happened next.
- Verify election document release: read the actual file, not the summary.
- How to fact check election claims: compare at least two sources from different sides.

## Source

| Question | Good sign | Bad sign |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who published it? | Named agency or court | Anonymous account |
| Is there a document? | A link to the file | "Trust me" |
| Is the number sourced? | Yes | "Only 25% revealed" with no source |

## Original text

1. **Open the file.**
2. **Find the exact sentence** being quoted.
3. **Read the paragraph around it.**
4. **Note "could" vs "did."**

## Independent review

1. **Read a supporter's take** and a critic's take.
2. **Check if an official or court responded.**
3. **Look for experts with no stake** in the outcome.

## Waiting

This isn't a "trust me" thing. This is "verify me."

1. **Give it 48 hours**: first reports are often wrong.
2. **Watch for corrections.**
3. **Look for consequences**: charges, rulings, audits.
4. **Ask what the source promised before**: see [promises made before](/blog/mike-lindell-election-evidence-promises/).
5. **Prepare your questions**: see [questions for the source](/blog/hidden-election-data-claims/).
