All right, guys, here's the quiet thief nobody talks about enough: inflation. Inflation and purchasing power move in opposite directions. When prices rise, each dollar buys less. The Bureau of Labor Statistics measures this with the Consumer Price Index [1]. At 3% inflation a year, prices double in about 24 years, so the same dollar buys about half as much. That's the math of erosion.

Here's what purchasing power is, the math of erosion, who is hit hardest and how savers respond.

**Quick answer**

- What is purchasing power: what your money can buy.
- How inflation affects savings: cash loses value each year.
- Inflation erodes savings: at 3%, buying power halves in about 24 years.
- Where to check: CPI data from BLS [1] and FRED [2].

## What is purchasing power?

How much a dollar buys. The Fed tries to keep inflation low and steady with its rate decisions. See [what a rate move means for you](/blog/fed-interest-rates-affect-economy/).

## The math of erosion

| Inflation per year | Buying power of $10,000 after 10 years | After 20 years |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2% | ~$8,200 | ~$6,700 |
| 3% | ~$7,400 | ~$5,500 |
| 5% | ~$6,100 | ~$3,800 |

Quick rule: divide 72 by the inflation rate to get the years for prices to double.

## Who is hit hardest?

1. **Savers in cash** earning less than inflation.
2. **People on fixed incomes.**
3. **Lower-income households,** who spend more of their money on essentials.

## How do savers respond?

This isn't a "trust me" thing. This is "verify me." Common approaches:

1. **Compare your savings rate** with the CPI [1].
2. **Use insured high-yield accounts** for short-term money.
3. **Invest long-term money** in a diversified mix.
4. **Avoid panic buys** pitched as "inflation-proof."
