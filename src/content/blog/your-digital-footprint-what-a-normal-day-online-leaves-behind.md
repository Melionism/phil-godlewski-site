All right, guys, let's walk through an ordinary day and count the traces. That's your digital footprint: the trail of data you leave every time you use a phone, a card, an app or a website. What is a digital footprint? Some of it is "active," like posts and photos you share on purpose. Most of it is "passive," collected without you noticing: location pings, device IDs, purchase records and metadata. A Stanford study of 823 volunteers' phone records showed that call and text metadata alone could reveal sensitive details such as medical conditions and gun ownership [1][2].

Here's a day of data, metadata, what stays permanent, how to shrink it and what companies do with it.

**Quick answer**

- What is a digital footprint: the data trail from your online and card activity.
- Digital footprint examples: location history, searches, purchases, message metadata.
- How to check your digital footprint: search yourself, download your data from big platforms, check data broker sites.
- Shrinking it: turn off what you don't need, delete what you can, opt out of brokers.

## Metadata

Metadata is data about data: who you contacted, when, for how long, from where. It doesn't include the words, but it can reveal more than the words. Former NSA general counsel Stewart Baker put it bluntly: "metadata absolutely tells you everything about somebody's life" [2]. Read [why contacts and timestamps reveal so much](/blog/metadata/).

## What stays permanent?

1. **Public posts** that others saved or screenshotted.
2. **Records held by companies**, depending on their retention policies.
3. **Data already sold** to brokers.
4. **Leaked data** from breaches.

What you can usually remove: your own posts, old accounts, many broker listings and some company records by request.

## How to shrink it

This isn't a "trust me" thing. This is "verify me."

1. **Search your name** in a private window and note what shows up.
2. **Download your data** from Google, Apple, Meta to see what they hold.
3. **Turn off location history** and ad personalization.
4. **Delete old accounts** you don't use.
5. **Opt out of data brokers.**
6. **Think before posting** details that answer security questions.

## What do companies do with it?

Mostly, they target ads and sell or share profiles. The FTC found one data broker had 3,000 data segments on nearly every US consumer [3]. Read [data brokers](/blog/data-brokers/).
