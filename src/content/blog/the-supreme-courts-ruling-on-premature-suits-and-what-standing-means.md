All right, guys, when the Supreme Court acted on Trump's mail ballot order in August 2026, I saw a lot of posts saying "SCOTUS approved it." That's not what happened. In Trump v. California, the Court stayed Judge Indira Talwani's injunction against EO 14399 [1]. The majority found the government likely to win on its argument that the states' claims weren't justiciable yet: it measured the states' injury at the time they sued, before the Postal Service had even proposed a rule, and found that harm too speculative [1]. It described the order as an internal directive that doesn't itself impose requirements on anyone outside the executive branch [1]. And it stressed that it was not ruling on whether the order is lawful [1]. Justices Sotomayor, Kagan and Jackson dissented separately [1]. A few weeks later, on September 14, the Court refused to let the Postal Service's actual rule take effect [2]. Both things are true: the order wasn't struck down, and it wasn't approved.

Here's the decision, why it isn't approval, standing, and ripeness.

**Quick answer**

- Supreme Court mail ballot order ripeness: the Court said the states sued too early [1].
- Not approval: the Court said it wasn't ruling on lawfulness [1].
- Standing: injury was too speculative when the suit was filed [1].
- Later: on September 14, the Court refused to let the USPS rule take effect [2].

## Standing

To sue, you must show a concrete injury caused by the defendant that a court can fix. The majority said the states' injury, as of when they filed, was too speculative [1].

## Ripeness

This isn't a "trust me" thing. This is "verify me."

A case is "ripe" when the harm is real enough to decide now. Once the Postal Service issued its actual rule on August 21, the states had something concrete to challenge, and that new fight went differently [2][3].
