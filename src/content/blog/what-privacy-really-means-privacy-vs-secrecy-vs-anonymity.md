All right, guys, before we fight for privacy, we need to know what we're fighting for. People use three words as if they mean the same thing: privacy, secrecy and anonymity. They don't. What is privacy? It's your control over who sees information about you and how it's used. Secrecy is keeping something hidden from everyone. Anonymity is acting without your name being attached. Mix them up and you end up either paranoid about everything or careless about what matters.

This matters because most of us already feel we've lost control. In a 2023 Pew Research Center survey, 73% of US adults said they have little or no control over the data companies collect about them, and 79% felt that way about the government [1]. If you don't know which of the three you need, you can't get it back.

Here are the three different ideas, the "nothing to hide" argument, the common myths and why the distinction matters.

**Quick answer**

- What is privacy: control over who sees your information and how it's used.
- Privacy vs anonymity: privacy hides the content or limits the audience; anonymity hides who you are.
- Difference between privacy and secrecy: secrecy means nobody knows; privacy means the right people know.
- Why it matters: each one needs different tools and habits.

## Three different ideas

| Idea | What it protects | Example | Typical tool |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Privacy | Who gets to see something | Your doctor sees your test results, your employer doesn't | Settings, laws, encryption |
| Secrecy | That something exists at all | A surprise party | Not telling anyone |
| Anonymity | Your identity, not the action | An anonymous tip | Pseudonyms, Tor |

Think about your mail. Everyone sees the envelope go into your mailbox. That's not private. What's inside, though, is for you. That's privacy. If nobody knew you even received a letter, that would be secrecy. If the letter had no return address, the sender would be anonymous.

Most of daily life needs privacy, not secrecy. Your bank knows your balance. Your pharmacist knows your prescriptions. The question isn't "does anyone know?" It's "do the right people know, and only them?" Start with the big picture in [digital privacy](/blog/digital-privacy/).

## The "nothing to hide" argument

You've heard it: "If you have nothing to hide, you have nothing to fear." It sounds strong until you notice it treats privacy like secrecy. It assumes the only reason to want privacy is to hide wrongdoing. But you close the bathroom door. You don't post your salary. You don't hand your diary to strangers. None of that is guilt.

Law professor Daniel Solove made this point in a well-known 2007 paper: the argument works only if you define privacy narrowly as hiding bad things, and most privacy harms aren't about that at all [2]. They're about data being collected, combined and used in ways you didn't agree to. Read [i have nothing to hide argument](/blog/i-have-nothing-hide-argument/).

## Common myths

| Myth | Reality |
| --- | --- |
| "Privacy is only for people with secrets" | Privacy is control, not guilt [2] |
| "Anonymous means private" | You can be anonymous and still expose content |
| "Private means nobody can ever see it" | Private means limited, chosen access |
| "It's too late, everything is already out there" | You can still limit new collection and remove some old data |
| "Only criminals use anonymity tools" | Journalists, abuse survivors and ordinary people use them |

I get it, I get it. When you've seen enough headlines, it's tempting to give up. But "some data is out there" isn't the same as "all data is out there." Read the full list in [ten common privacy claims checked](/blog/privacy-blackmail-myths/).

## Why does the distinction matter?

Because you can't protect everything equally, and you shouldn't try. Here's how the three ideas turn into different choices:

1. **You need privacy** for medical, financial and family information. That means good passwords, careful app permissions and knowing your legal rights.
2. **You need secrecy** only for a few things, like passwords and recovery codes. Those should be known by no one but you.
3. **You need anonymity** in specific situations, like reporting abuse or researching something sensitive. That takes different tools and more effort.

This isn't a "trust me" thing. This is "verify me." Write down three things you want to keep private, one thing you need to keep secret, and one situation where you'd want to be anonymous. That list is the start of a personal threat model: read [the first question to ask](/blog/threat-modeling-ordinary-people/).

## FAQ

**What is privacy?** Control over who sees information about you and how it's used.

**What's the difference between privacy and anonymity?** Privacy limits who sees the content; anonymity hides who you are.

**What's the difference between privacy and secrecy?** Secrecy means nobody knows; privacy means only the right people know.

**Do I need anonymity in daily life?** Rarely; most people mainly need privacy.

**Is privacy a lost cause?** No; most people feel little control [1], but you can still limit collection.
