All right, guys, an executive order tells the government what to do. A regulation tells the rest of us exactly how it works. For EO 13848, that regulation is 31 CFR Part 579, the "Foreign Interference in U.S. Elections Sanctions Regulations." OFAC released it on April 26, 2019, and it took effect on publication in the Federal Register on April 29, 2019 (84 FR 17951) [1][2]. It was published "in abbreviated form for the purpose of providing immediate guidance to the public," with OFAC saying it intended to add more detailed rules later [2]. It has been amended several times since, including in December 2022, in September and December 2024, and in July 2026 [2]. Appendix A reprints EO 13848 itself [2].

Here's what the rule does, the entry-ban authority, EO 13694 and how to read it.

**Quick answer**

- 31 CFR Part 579: Treasury's regulations implementing EO 13848 [1][2].
- Effective: April 29, 2019 [1].
- Core: prohibits dealings in blocked property of designated foreign persons [2].
- Exceptions: licenses for legal services, emergency medical care, official business, NGOs and some humanitarian goods [2].
- Penalties: under IEEPA section 206 [2].

## Entry-ban authority

The visa and entry ban comes from Section 6 of the order and immigration law section 212(f), not from Part 579 [3]. Part 579 deals with property and transactions; the State Department and DHS handle entry.

## EO 13694

Some election-related targets are also listed under the cyber sanctions order, EO 13694. That one has its own regulations, 31 CFR Part 578. When a press release cites several orders, check each [4].

## How to read it

This isn't a "trust me" thing. This is "verify me."

1. **Start with § 579.201**: what's prohibited.
2. **Read the definitions** in Subpart C.
3. **Check Subpart E** for what's allowed by license.
4. **Look at the amendment dates** on eCFR.
