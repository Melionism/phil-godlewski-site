All right, guys, "Do you have a rewards card?" You hear it at every register. Loyalty cards and privacy go together like a coupon and its fine print: the store gives you a discount, and in return gets a detailed record of everything you buy, when and where. California's privacy law actually treats loyalty programs as "financial incentives": businesses must explain what data they collect in exchange and how they value it [1]. And retail data is one of the sources data brokers use to build profiles; the FTC found one broker's database covered one trillion dollars in consumer transactions [2].

Here's how loyalty programs work, what they collect, the trade-off and how to use them more safely.

**Quick answer**

- Loyalty cards privacy: discounts in exchange for your purchase history.
- What they collect: items, times, stores, sometimes linked to your phone and email.
- Who gets it: the retailer, partners and sometimes data brokers [2].
- Safer use: minimal sign-up details, skip it for sensitive purchases.

## How do loyalty programs work?

| You give | You get |
| --- | --- |
| Name, phone, email | Member prices |
| Every purchase recorded | Points, coupons |
| Sometimes location via the app | Personalized offers |
| Permission to share with partners | Partner deals |

It's one piece of [what purchase history reveals](/blog/purchase-history-reveals/).

## What do they collect?

1. **What you bought**, down to brand and size.
2. **When and where** you shopped.
3. **How you paid.**
4. **App data**, like location, if you use the store's app.
5. **Inferences**, like household size or health interests [2].

## The trade-off

I get it, I get it. Groceries are expensive and that discount helps. The question is whether the trade is worth it for you. In California, stores must give a "notice of financial incentive" explaining the deal [1]. Elsewhere, read the program's privacy policy for words like "share," "partners" and "sell."

## How to use them more safely

This isn't a "trust me" thing. This is "verify me."

1. **Give the minimum:** phone number only, if possible.
2. **Don't install the store app** unless you need it; if you do, deny location.
3. **Skip the card** for pharmacy and sensitive items.
4. **Opt out of sale or sharing** where your state allows [1].
5. **Request deletion** if you stop using the program.

And if you think paying in crypto avoids all this, read [a public ledger by design](/blog/are-crypto-purchases-anonymous/).

## A closer look: the "data dividend"

Some programs openly say they share data with partners for marketing. That's legal in most places if disclosed. Your leverage is choice: use the program where the savings are worth it, and skip it where the data is too personal.

## FAQ

**Do loyalty cards track me?** Yes, they record your purchases.

**Is the discount worth the privacy cost?** It depends on what you buy and how often.

**Do stores sell loyalty card data?** Some share or sell it to partners [2].

**What does California require?** A notice explaining the financial incentive [1].

**How do I protect myself?** Give minimal details and skip it for sensitive items.
