All right, guys, what is a circuit breaker in trading? It's an emergency brake. When the S&P 500 falls sharply in one day, exchanges pause all trading so people can breathe. The market-wide levels are a 7% drop (Level 1), 13% (Level 2) and 20% (Level 3), measured from the prior day's close [1]. Circuit breakers exist because of 1987: after the Dow fell 22.6% on October 19, exchanges adopted rules to pause trading in big declines [2].

Here's what they are, the trigger levels, past use and whether they help.

**Quick answer**

- Market-wide circuit breaker: a pause after big S&P 500 drops [1].
- Trigger levels: 7%, 13%, 20% [1].
- Trading halts explained: Level 1 and 2 pause trading for 15 minutes earlier in the day; Level 3 ends trading for the day.
- Origin: reforms after Black Monday 1987 [2].

## What are they?

A pause button for the whole market. They're part of [the anatomy of a sell-off](/blog/stock-market-crashes-happen/).

## Trigger levels

| Level | S&P 500 drop | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 7% | 15-minute halt (if before 3:25 p.m. ET) |
| 2 | 13% | 15-minute halt (if before 3:25 p.m. ET) |
| 3 | 20% | Trading stops for the day |

Levels from NYSE Rule 7.12 [1].

## Past use

The market-wide breakers were triggered several times in March 2020, as COVID hit. Individual stocks also have their own pauses for sudden moves.

## Do they help?

I get it, I get it. Some people say halts just delay the selling. Supporters say they stop panic from feeding on itself and give buyers time to show up. After 1987, the Fed's history notes exchanges had "no power to halt trading" during heavy selling, and that was one of the flaws fixed [2].

This isn't a "trust me" thing. This is "verify me." A halt protects the market, not your account balance. For that, see [what SIPC covers and what it doesn't](/blog/sipc-protect-brokerage-accounts/).
