All right, guys, everyone in politics makes predictions: pollsters, betting markets, TV pundits, streamers, me. Most people never go back and check them. Here's a simple method that works for all of them. A useful prediction has three parts: a clear claim, a deadline, and a way to tell whether it happened. "Something big is coming" fails all three. "The Senate will pass the SAVE America Act before November 3, 2026" passes all three, and we can check it: it didn't pass, and the Senate's last pre-election attempt failed 52-46 on August 8 [1]. Pollsters and markets get checked too. Before 2026, the historical baseline was that the president's party loses House seats in almost every midterm, 18 of 20 since 1946 [2]. Anyone predicting the opposite needed a strong reason.

Here are deadlines, clarity, keeping score, and warnings.

**Quick answer**

- How to evaluate political predictions: check clarity, deadline and outcome.
- Deadline: a date the claim must happen by.
- Clarity: wording specific enough that you'd know if it failed.
- Score: keep a running record of hits and misses.
- Baseline: compare with what history alone would have predicted [2].

## Deadlines

A prediction without a date can never fail, so it can never be tested. Ask: "By when?" If the answer keeps moving, that tells you something. A scorecard of the July predictions: [a scorecard of the July predictions](/blog/blogger-election-predictions-fact-check/).

## Clarity

| Vague | Clear |
| --- | --- |
| "Big things are coming" | "X will be indicted by June 30" |
| "The bill will move" | "The Senate will pass it before Nov 3" |
| "Watch this space" | "Republicans will keep the House" |

## Keeping score

This isn't a "trust me" thing. This is "verify me."

1. **Write it down** with the date you heard it.
2. **Record the deadline.**
3. **Score it** when the date passes: hit, miss or partial.
4. **Count misses** as well as hits.
5. **Compare** with a simple baseline, like the historical midterm pattern [2].

## Warnings

- **Moved deadlines**: "it was always going to be later."
- **Reinterpretation**: "what I really meant was…"
- **Only hits remembered**: misses quietly dropped.
- **Unfalsifiable claims**: nothing could ever prove them wrong.

## FAQ

**How do I evaluate a political prediction?** Check clarity, deadline and outcome.

**What makes a prediction useful?** It can clearly fail.

**Should I trust pollsters?** Check their track record like anyone else's.

**What's a baseline?** What history alone suggests, like midterm losses [2].

**What if the deadline keeps moving?** Count it as a miss.
