All right, guys, your health information might be the most personal data you have. So who can see your medical records? Your doctors, your clinic, your pharmacy and your health insurer, under the federal law called HIPAA. But HIPAA only covers certain organizations. The moment your health data goes to an app that isn't one of them, the protection can vanish. The US Department of Health and Human Services says that once a covered entity sends data to such an app at your request, the information "is no longer subject to the protections of the HIPAA Rules" [1]. And even covered data leaks: the 2024 Change Healthcare breach affected about 192.7 million people [2].

Here's what HIPAA covers, health apps, your rights and practical protection.

**Quick answer**

- Who has access to my health information: providers, insurers, pharmacies and their contractors, under HIPAA.
- Can insurance see medical records: your health insurer can see claims-related records.
- Medical records privacy gaps: many apps and devices aren't covered by HIPAA [1].
- Your rights: get a copy, ask for corrections, see who your data was shared with.

## What does HIPAA cover?

| Covered by HIPAA | Usually not covered |
| --- | --- |
| Doctors, hospitals, clinics | Fitness trackers |
| Health insurers | Period and pregnancy apps |
| Pharmacies | Most mental health and wellness apps |
| Their contractors ("business associates") | DNA testing companies |
| Health data you send to an app yourself | Not covered once it's there [1] |

Read [what does hipaa protect](/blog/hipaa-protect/).

## Health apps

Fitness, period, sleep and therapy apps collect sensitive data, often under privacy policies rather than HIPAA. In 2023 the FTC said the online therapy company BetterHelp shared people's email addresses, IP addresses and health questionnaire answers with platforms like Facebook and Snapchat for advertising; it agreed to pay $7.8 million [3]. ## Your rights

Under HIPAA, you generally have the right to:

1. **Get a copy** of your records.
2. **Ask for corrections.**
3. **Get a list** of certain disclosures.
4. **Ask for limits** on sharing, in some cases.
5. **File a complaint** with HHS if your rights were violated [4].

## Practical protection

This isn't a "trust me" thing. This is "verify me."

1. **Use your patient portal** with a strong password and two-factor login.
2. **Read app policies** before connecting health records.
3. **Check your insurer's "explanation of benefits"** for services you didn't get.
4. **Limit health details** you share in non-medical apps.
5. **Know your bank sees health clues too:** pharmacy and clinic payments show up in [what your bank knows about you](/blog/your-bank-knows-about-you/).

## A closer look: why breaches matter

Medical data can't be changed like a password. A diagnosis stays a diagnosis. That's why breaches like Change Healthcare are so serious: 192.7 million people's data was exposed [2].

## FAQ

**Who can see my medical records?** Providers, insurers, pharmacies and their contractors.

**Who has access to my health information?** Those covered entities, plus anyone you share it with.

**Can my insurance see my medical records?** Records tied to claims, generally yes.

**Does HIPAA cover health apps?** Usually not [1].

**What are my rights?** Copy, correction, disclosure list, complaint [4].

## What to do next

1. Turn on two-factor login for your patient portal.
2. Review which apps have your health data.
3. Request a copy of your records once a year.
