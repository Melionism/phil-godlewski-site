All right, guys, one part of the SAVE America Act gets less attention than voter ID but matters a lot to the people who run elections. The bill would make it a crime for an election official to register an applicant to vote in a federal election without the documentary proof of citizenship the bill requires [1][2]. That applies even if the applicant turns out to be a U.S. citizen [2]. It would also be a crime for executive branch officials to knowingly give material help to noncitizens trying to register or vote, and it would let private individuals sue officials who register voters without the required proof [1]. Supporters say officials should face consequences for breaking the rules. Critics, including election administrators, say the standard of what counts as acceptable proof is vague and that fear of prosecution will push workers to reject borderline applicants [2]. And no election-office budget comes with it [3].

Here's the text, what critics say, what supporters say, and how it would apply.

**Quick answer**

- SAVE act penalties election workers: criminal liability for registering someone without required proof [1][2].
- Even if the person is a citizen: yes, per the bill's design [2].
- Lawsuits: private individuals could sue officials [1].
- Criticism: vague standards and pressure on workers [2].

## The text

| Provision | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Registering without required proof | Criminal offense for the official [1][2] |
| Helping noncitizens register or vote | Crime for executive officials who knowingly do it [1] |
| Private right of action | Individuals can sue officials [1] |
| Alternative evidence process | States must create one, with an attestation under penalty of perjury [1] |

## What critics say

1. **Vagueness**: officials' discretion in the alternative-evidence process is a "gray area," as one expert told PBS [2].
2. **Chilling effect**: workers may refuse applicants to protect themselves.
3. **Staffing**: many offices are already short-handed, and the bill adds no money [3].

## What supporters say

- Officials who ignore the law should face consequences, like in other fields.
- Penalties make the citizenship rule real, not optional.
- Private lawsuits let citizens enforce it when officials won't.

## How it would apply

This isn't a "trust me" thing. This is "verify me."

The bill never became law, so no one has been charged under it [4]. If it ever passes, how prosecutors and courts read "required proof" would decide how hard it bites. Until then, election workers' personal details shouldn't be posted or targeted by anyone, whatever side they're on.
