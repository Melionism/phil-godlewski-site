All right, guys, Kansas is the best real-world test of a proof-of-citizenship law, because it ran one statewide for years. The Secure and Fair Elections Act, passed in 2011, required every new voter to show a passport, birth certificate or similar document; it took effect in 2013 [1]. More than 30,000 Kansans were blocked from registering or had registrations suspended [1]. In Fish v. Kobach, U.S. District Judge Julie Robinson ruled on June 18, 2018, that the law violated the Equal Protection Clause and the National Voter Registration Act [2]. She found that "at most, 67 noncitizens registered or attempted to register in Kansas over the last 19 years," and she ordered then-Secretary of State Kris Kobach, who argued the case, to take six extra hours of legal education for discovery violations [2]. The 10th Circuit upheld the ruling in 2020, and the appellate opinion counted 39 confirmed noncitizen registrations from 1999 to 2013 [1]. The state paid $1.9 million in attorneys' fees [1].

Here's the law, who was blocked, Fish v. Kobach, and lessons.

**Quick answer**

- Kansas proof of citizenship law: in effect 2013–2016, struck down in 2018 [1][2].
- Blocked: more than 30,000 people [1].
- Noncitizens found: at most 67 registered or attempted over 19 years [2].
- Appeal: 10th Circuit upheld in 2020 [1].

## Who was blocked

Many were people who registered at the DMV, where clerks weren't allowed to ask for citizenship documents or tell people about the requirement [1].
