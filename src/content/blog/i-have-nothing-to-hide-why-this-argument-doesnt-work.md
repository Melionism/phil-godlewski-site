All right, guys, here's a line I hear all the time, sometimes from people I love: "I have nothing to hide, so why should I care about privacy?" The nothing to hide argument sounds like common sense. It's actually built on a mistake. It assumes the only reason to want privacy is to cover up wrongdoing. Law professor Daniel Solove spent a whole paper on this and showed that privacy harms are mostly about collection, combination and misuse of data, not about hiding crimes [1].

And most people do care, even if they say otherwise. In a 2023 Pew survey, 71% of US adults were concerned about how the government uses data about them, up from 64% in 2019, and 73% felt they had little or no control over what companies collect [2].

Here's the argument, why it fails, everyday examples and a better question to ask.

**Quick answer**

- Nothing to hide argument: "if you've done nothing wrong, privacy doesn't matter."
- Why it fails: privacy is about control, not guilt [1].
- Nothing to hide privacy examples: medical records, salary, location, messages.
- Better question: "who gets my data, and what can they do with it?"

## The argument

It usually goes like this: surveillance only hurts bad people, good people have nothing to fear, so let them watch. It's often said in good faith by people who simply haven't been on the wrong end of a data leak or a mistaken record. It mixes up privacy with secrecy, which we covered in [how privacy differs from secrecy](/blog/privacy-vs-secrecy-vs-anonymity/).

## Why does it fail?

Five reasons:

1. **It defines privacy too narrowly.** Privacy isn't hiding bad things; it's deciding who sees what [1].
2. **Data gets combined.** Harmless pieces, like where you shop and when you sleep, add up to a detailed profile. The FTC found one data broker held 3,000 data segments on nearly every US consumer [3].
3. **Rules change.** What's legal and acceptable today may not be tomorrow, but the data stays.
4. **Mistakes happen.** Wrong records can cost you a job, a loan or an insurance rate.
5. **Others decide what's "wrong."** You don't get to define what someone else finds suspicious or embarrassing.

## Everyday examples

| You have nothing to hide, but... | Why you'd still want privacy |
| --- | --- |
| Your health | Insurers, employers or scammers could use it |
| Your salary | Coworkers, landlords, and sales pitches |
| Your location | Stalkers, burglars, an abusive ex |
| Your messages | Context gets lost when read by strangers |
| Your purchases | Data brokers infer health or money trouble [3] |
| Your passwords | Obviously |

I get it, I get it. You're an honest person. That's exactly why scammers, data brokers and bad actors like your data: honest people are easier to target when everything about them is known.

## A better question

This isn't a "trust me" thing. This is "verify me." Instead of "do I have something to hide?", ask:

1. **Who collects my data?**
2. **What do they do with it?**
3. **Who else can get it?**
4. **What happens if it leaks?**

Answer those four and you'll know where to put your effort. Then check the myths: [a short list with verdicts on privacy](/blog/privacy-blackmail-myths/).

## FAQ

**What is the nothing to hide argument?** The claim that privacy only matters to people doing something wrong.

**Why doesn't the nothing to hide argument work?** It confuses privacy with secrecy and ignores how data gets combined and misused [1].

**Why does privacy matter if you have nothing to hide?** Because others decide how your data is used [3].

**Do most people care about privacy?** Yes; most feel little control over their data [2].

**What should I ask instead?** Who collects my data, and what can they do with it?
