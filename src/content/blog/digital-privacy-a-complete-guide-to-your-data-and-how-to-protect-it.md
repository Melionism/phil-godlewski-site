All right, guys, let's talk about something that touches every one of us, every day: digital privacy. What is digital privacy? It's your ability to decide who sees information about you online: what you say, where you go, what you buy, who you talk to. And most people feel they've lost that control. In a 2023 Pew Research Center survey, 73% of US adults said they have little or no control over the data companies collect about them, and 79% said the same about the government [1]. Sixty-seven percent said they understand little or nothing about what companies do with their data [1].

I get it, I get it. It feels like the battle is already lost. It isn't. You can't disappear from the internet, but you can decide what matters most, close the biggest holes and stop handing out data you don't need to. This online privacy guide walks you through it.

Here's what privacy really means, your threat model, your digital footprint, who collects your data and where to start.

**Quick answer**

- What is digital privacy: control over who sees your information online.
- Most people feel they've lost it: 73% say they have little or no control over company data collection [1].
- How to protect your privacy online: know what you're protecting, then lock down accounts, phone and browser.
- Where to start: passwords, two-factor login, app permissions, data broker opt-outs.

## What does privacy really mean?

Privacy isn't hiding something bad. It's choosing what to share and with whom. You close the bathroom door, and nobody calls that suspicious. Privacy, secrecy and anonymity are three different things, and mixing them up leads to bad decisions:

| Idea | What it means | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Privacy | You control who sees it | Your medical records go to your doctor, not your boss |
| Secrecy | Nobody is supposed to know it exists | A surprise party, a trade secret |
| Anonymity | People see the action, not who did it | An anonymous tip line |

Read [privacy vs secrecy vs anonymity](/blog/privacy-vs-secrecy-vs-anonymity/).

## What's your threat model?

That sounds technical. It isn't. A threat model is a short list of answers to plain questions. The Electronic Frontier Foundation's security planning guide uses six [2]:

1. What do I want to protect?
2. Who do I want to protect it from?
3. How bad are the consequences if I fail?
4. How likely is it that I will need to protect it?
5. How much trouble am I willing to go through?
6. Who are my allies?

A retiree worried about phone scams and a journalist worried about a foreign government need very different plans. I need you to answer these for yourself before you buy any privacy tool. Read [threat modeling for ordinary people](/blog/threat-modeling-ordinary-people/).

## What is your digital footprint?

Every search, every app you open, every card swipe and every step your phone counts leaves a trace. Some you leave on purpose, like posts and photos. Most you leave without noticing: location pings, metadata on messages, purchase records, cookies in your browser.

| Activity | What it leaves behind |
| --- | --- |
| Morning news app | Reading habits, device ID, location |
| Card at the coffee shop | Time, place, amount |
| Driving with maps open | A minute-by-minute route |
| Messaging a friend | Who, when, how often (metadata) |
| Evening streaming | Viewing history, interests |

Read [what a normal day online leaves behind](/blog/digital-footprint/).

## Who collects your data?

Three groups, mainly:

1. **Apps and platforms** you use directly.
2. **Advertisers and trackers** inside those apps and websites.
3. **Data brokers** you've never heard of.

That third group surprises people the most. The Federal Trade Commission studied nine data brokers and found one held 3,000 data segments on nearly every US consumer; another added three billion new records every month [3]. The FTC said this happens largely without consumers' knowledge, because brokers don't collect from you directly [3]. They also build sensitive categories, like health interests, from what you buy and search [3].

And yes, governments request data too. In the same Pew survey, 71% of Americans said they were concerned about how the government uses their data, up from 64% in 2019 [1]. Read [what data do tech companies collect](/blog/data-tech-companies-collect/).

## Where to start

This isn't a "trust me" thing. This is "verify me." You don't need to be a hacker. You need one evening and a list:

| Step | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Use a password manager | One stolen password stops unlocking everything |
| Turn on two-factor login | CISA says it makes you much less likely to be hacked [4] |
| Review app permissions | Location, microphone and contacts are the big three |
| Tighten browser settings | Fewer trackers following you around |
| Opt out of data brokers | Shrinks the profile others can buy |
| Check if your data leaked | Know which passwords to change first |

Do these first, before any paid product. Most of them are free. Read the full [privacy checklist](/blog/privacy-checklist/).

## FAQ

**What is digital privacy?** Control over who sees your information online.

**Why does online privacy matter if I have nothing to hide?** Privacy is about control, not guilt; data can be used for scams, pricing and profiling [3].

**How do I protect my privacy online?** Start with passwords, two-factor login, permissions and broker opt-outs [4].

**Who collects the most data about me?** Apps, ad trackers and data brokers [3].

**Do most people feel in control of their data?** No: 73% say they have little or no control over company collection [1].
