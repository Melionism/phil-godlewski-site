All right, guys, here's a new piece of the puzzle. Deepfakes make "it's a movie" feel more possible than ever. In January 2024, robocalls used an AI-generated clone of Biden's voice to tell New Hampshire voters to "save your vote for the November election." The FCC proposed a $6 million fine against the consultant behind them [1].

That's a real case of AI faking a president. It cuts both ways: fakes can fool people, and real videos can be waved off as fakes. Here's what deepfakes can do, the "liar's dividend," examples in politics and how to check a video.

## What can deepfakes do?

They can make a person appear to say or do what they never did. The 2024 robocall used an AI voice clone and spoofed a real local consultant's number so it looked legitimate [1]. The FCC says calls using AI-generated voices count as "artificial" under federal telephone law [1].

## What is the liar's dividend?

It's the flip side. Once everyone knows fakes exist, anyone caught on a real video can say "that's AI." The real video loses its power. I need you to watch for both moves: a fake passed off as real, and a real clip waved off as fake.

## What are the examples in politics?

The 2024 robocall is the best documented, because a regulator named the person and set a fine [1]. Others show up every election season: altered clips of candidates, AI images of events that never happened, and real clips called fake.

## How do you check a video?

This isn't a "trust me" thing. This is "verify me."

1. **Find the first upload.** Who posted it first, and when?
2. **Look for other angles.** Real events get filmed by many people.
3. **Check the details.** Hands, teeth, ears, text in the background, lip sync.
4. **Look for an official record.** A transcript, a schedule, a statement.
5. **Ask who gains** if you believe it.
