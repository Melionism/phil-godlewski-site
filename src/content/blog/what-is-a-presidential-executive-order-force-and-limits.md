All right, guys, we throw around "executive order" like it's a magic wand. It isn't. The National Archives defines executive orders as "official documents, numbered consecutively, through which the President of the United States manages the operations of the Federal Government" [1]. Each one gets a number from the Office of the Federal Register, gets published in the Federal Register, and is compiled in Title 3 of the Code of Federal Regulations [1]. An order has the force of law when it rests on the Constitution or on a statute Congress passed. When it goes beyond that, courts can strike it down. The classic case is Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer in 1952, when the Supreme Court ruled 6-3 that President Truman couldn't seize the steel mills without authority from Congress or the Constitution [2]. Courts did the same in 2025 and 2026 with parts of the March 2025 election order [3].

Here's the definition, legal force, limits and how they end.

**Quick answer**

- What is an executive order: a numbered presidential directive to the federal government [1].
- Legal force: only as strong as the constitutional or statutory authority behind it [2].
- Limits: can't override the Constitution or a law; courts can block it [2][3].
- How they end: revoked by any later president, overridden by Congress, or struck down in court.

## Definition

| Document | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| Executive order | Numbered directive managing the federal government [1] |
| Proclamation | Often ceremonial, sometimes legal (e.g., entry bans) |
| Memorandum | Directive, not always numbered or published |
| Notice | e.g., continuing an emergency |

## Legal force

An order binds federal agencies. It works on private people only through a law it implements. EO 13848, for example, rests on IEEPA, the National Emergencies Act and immigration law [4].

## Limits

1. **The Constitution**: e.g., Article I gives election rules to states and Congress.
2. **Statutes**: an order can't contradict a law.
3. **Courts**: Youngstown in 1952 [2]; the March 2025 election order in 2025 and 2026 [3].
4. **Money**: Congress controls funding.

## How they end

This isn't a "trust me" thing. This is "verify me."

- **Revoked** by the same or a later president.
- **Overridden** by a law, signed or passed over a veto.
- **Blocked** by a court.
- **Expired**, if the order has an end date or depends on an emergency that ends.

## FAQ

**What is an executive order?** A numbered presidential directive to the federal government [1].

**Is it a law?** No, but it can carry legal force through a statute.

**Can a court block one?** Yes [2][3].

**Can the next president cancel it?** Yes.

**Where are they published?** In the Federal Register [1].
