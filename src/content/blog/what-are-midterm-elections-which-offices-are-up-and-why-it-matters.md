All right, guys, midterms are the elections held halfway through a president's four-year term. The President isn't on the ballot, but almost everything else in Washington is. All 435 seats in the House of Representatives are up, because House members serve two-year terms [1]. About a third of the Senate is up, because senators serve six-year terms in staggered classes [1]. Many states also elect governors, legislatures and local officials; in 2026, 36 states elected governors [2]. The result decides who controls Congress for the second half of a presidency. On November 3, 2026, Democrats won control of both the House and the Senate, according to the Associated Press [2]. History says that's not unusual: from 1946 to 2022, the president's party lost House seats in 18 of 20 midterms, by an average of 26 seats [3].

Here are the basics, how the president's party usually fares, key dates to 2029, and why they matter.

**Quick answer**

- What are midterm elections: federal elections in the middle of a presidential term [1].
- On the ballot: all 435 House seats, about a third of the Senate, many governors [1][2].
- Pattern: the president's party usually loses House seats; average loss of 26 since 1946 [3].
- 2026: Democrats won both chambers, per AP [2].

## How the president's party usually fares

Since 1946, only twice did the president's party gain House seats in a midterm: 1998 and 2002 [3]. The biggest losses were 64 seats in 2010, 54 in 1994 and 42 in 2018 [3]. The full record: [president party midterm losses history](/blog/president-party-midterm-losses-history/).

## Why they matter

This isn't a "trust me" thing. This is "verify me."

- **Control of Congress** decides which bills move and which investigations open.
- **The Senate** confirms judges and cabinet members.
- **The budget** runs through the House.
- **States** set election rules, so governors and legislatures matter for 2028.
