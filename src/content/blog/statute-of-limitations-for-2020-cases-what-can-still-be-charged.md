All right, guys, here's the hard truth about prosecuting 2020: the clock. Under federal law, unless a statute says otherwise, no one can be prosecuted for a non-capital crime unless the indictment is filed within five years of the offense, under 18 U.S.C. § 3282. Conduct from November 2020 hit that five-year mark in November 2025. That's exactly what a Trump-appointed federal judge in Georgia, William Ray II, pointed to in July 2026 when he blocked a Justice Department subpoena for the personal information of Fulton County's 2020 election workers: he wrote that the statute of limitations for any possible 2020 crime "has long expired" [1]. Election experts made the same point after the FBI search of Fulton County's election office in January 2026, noting that the relevant statutes typically carry a five-year limit [2].

Here's the five-year rule, the exceptions, conspiracy and what's left.

**Quick answer**

- Statute of limitations 2020 election crimes: generally five years, so most expired by late 2025 [1][2].
- Election crime statute of limitations: five years for most federal crimes, under 18 U.S.C. § 3282.
- Federal five year statute of limitations: runs from when the crime was completed.
- 2020 election charges deadline: possible only for later conduct, continuing conspiracies or specific exceptions.

## Exceptions

1. **Some crimes have longer limits**, such as certain fraud or terrorism offenses.
2. **Fugitives**: the clock can pause for someone fleeing justice.
3. **Later conduct**: lying to investigators in 2023 or 2025 is a new crime with a new clock.
4. **Sealed indictments** filed before the deadline count.

## Conspiracy

For a conspiracy, the five years usually run from the last overt act in furtherance of it. If prosecutors can show a conspiracy continued, with acts after 2020, the deadline moves. They have to prove that, though.

## What's left

This isn't a "trust me" thing. This is "verify me." For most 2020 election-day conduct, the federal window is closed. What remains: later conduct, cover-up acts, false statements, and anything charged under statutes with longer limits. State limits vary. ## FAQ

**Are there exceptions?** Yes: longer statutes, fugitives, continuing conspiracies, later acts.

**Can someone be charged for lying later?** Yes, new conduct, new clock.
