All right, guys, you've heard the ads on every podcast and video: "Get a VPN and become invisible online." So, do you need a VPN? Sometimes. A VPN, or virtual private network, sends your internet traffic through an encrypted tunnel to the VPN company's server. That hides which sites you visit from your internet provider and from whoever runs the Wi-Fi you're on, and it hides your real IP address from the sites you visit. What it doesn't do: make you anonymous, stop tracking when you're logged in, or protect you from scams. The Electronic Frontier Foundation's guide puts it simply: with a VPN, you're shifting trust from your provider to the VPN company [1].

Here's what a VPN does, when it helps, the misleading claims and how to choose one.

**Quick answer**

- What does a VPN protect: your traffic from your ISP and local network, and your IP from websites.
- Is a VPN worth it: yes on public Wi-Fi or to hide browsing from your provider; less so at home on secure sites.
- VPN myths: "total anonymity," "hacker-proof," "blocks all tracking."
- The trade-off: the VPN company can see what your ISP used to see [1].

## What does a VPN do?

| Without a VPN | With a VPN |
| --- | --- |
| Your ISP sees which sites you connect to | The VPN company sees them instead [1] |
| Sites see your home IP address | Sites see the VPN server's address |
| Public Wi-Fi owner sees your connections | They see only a connection to the VPN |
| Logged-in sites know it's you | Still know it's you |
| Advertising IDs on your phone work | Still work |

Phone advertising IDs and cookies don't care about your IP address. That's why a VPN alone doesn't stop the kind of tracking described in [what advertising IDs reveal](/blog/ad-tracking-explained/).

## When does it help?

1. **Public Wi-Fi** at airports, hotels and cafes.
2. **Hiding browsing from your internet provider**, which in the US isn't bound by the FCC's 2016 broadband privacy rules, repealed in 2017 [2].
3. **Hiding your IP address** from sites, for example to avoid revealing your city.
4. **Travel**, to reach home services.

## Misleading claims

| Claim | Reality |
| --- | --- |
| "Military-grade encryption makes you untraceable" | Encryption protects traffic in transit; it doesn't erase accounts, cookies or device IDs |
| "No-logs, guaranteed" | Only as good as the company's honesty and any independent audit |
| "Stops all hackers" | Doesn't stop phishing, malware or weak passwords |
| "Complete anonymity" | Logged-in services and payment info still identify you |
| "Free and unlimited" | Free VPNs often make money from your data or ads |

I get it, I get it. The ads make it sound like a magic shield. If a product promises absolute protection, that's a red flag in itself.

## How to choose

This isn't a "trust me" thing. This is "verify me." The EFF suggests looking at who runs the VPN, where it's based, how it makes money and whether its claims have been checked [1].

1. **Independent audit** of the no-logs claim, with the report public.
2. **Clear business model:** you pay, so you're not the product.
3. **Known company** with a real address and leadership.
4. **Kill switch** so traffic doesn't leak if the connection drops.
5. **Avoid free VPNs** from unknown developers.

And don't forget the basics, which matter more: a few settings worth changing in [a few settings worth changing](/blog/browser-privacy-settings/), and what private browsing really does in [what that dark window does](/blog/incognito-mode-hide/).

## A real-world example

Picture this: you're at an airport, connected to "Free_Airport_WiFi." Without a VPN, the network owner, or someone who set up a fake network with that name, can see which sites your phone connects to. With a reputable VPN, they see only that you're connected to a VPN. That's a real benefit. Now picture you're at home, logged into Google, shopping on Amazon. A VPN changes very little there: both companies still know exactly who you are.

## FAQ

**Do I need a VPN?** On public Wi-Fi or to hide browsing from your provider, it helps.

**What does a VPN protect?** Your traffic from your ISP and local network, and your IP address from sites.

**Is a VPN worth it?** Yes for specific needs; it's not a full privacy solution.

**What are the biggest VPN myths?** Total anonymity and blocking all tracking.

**Are free VPNs safe?** Many aren't; check how they make money [1].

## What to do next

1. Decide whether you're often on public Wi-Fi.
2. If yes, pick a paid VPN with a public audit.
3. Fix your passwords and two-factor login first.
