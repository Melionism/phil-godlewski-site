All right, guys, how does the government get data from tech companies? Usually not by hacking. It asks, with legal paperwork. Government data requests come in a few forms: a subpoena, a court order, a search warrant, or national security requests. Each one reaches a different kind of data. And the courts have drawn lines: in Carpenter v. United States (2018), the Supreme Court ruled that police generally need a warrant to get historical cell-phone location records [1]. Big companies publish transparency reports showing how many requests they get and how often they hand over data [2][3].

Here's how requests work, Five Eyes, transparency reports and the limits.

**Quick answer**

- Lawful access to data: subpoenas, court orders, warrants and national security requests.
- Subpoena vs warrant data: subpoenas usually get basic account info; warrants are needed for content and, per Carpenter, historical location [1].
- How government gets data from tech companies: by sending legal process to the company.
- Check: Apple and Google publish transparency reports [2][3].

## How do requests work?

| Request type | Who approves | What it usually gets |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Subpoena | A prosecutor or agency | Name, email, payment info, IP logs |
| Court order (2703(d)) | A judge, lower standard | Non-content records like logs |
| Search warrant | A judge, probable cause | Message content, photos, location history [1] |
| National security requests | FISA court or a letter | Varies; often secret |
| Emergency request | The company decides | When life is in danger |

## Transparency reports

Apple and Google publish reports on government requests: how many came in, from which countries, and how often they produced data [2][3]. These reports don't show everything; national security requests are often reported only in broad ranges. But they're the best public window we have.

## Limits

This isn't a "trust me" thing. This is "verify me."

1. **Encryption limits what companies can hand over.** If a company can't read your end-to-end encrypted messages, it can't give them up.
2. **Courts limit what police can get** without a warrant [1].
3. **Data you give third-party apps** may be easier to get; see [the login you hand over](/blog/fintech-app-privacy/).
4. **Companies can push back**, and some do.

## A closer look: what you can control

You can't stop a valid warrant. You can decide what data exists. Turn off location history you don't need, use end-to-end encrypted messaging, and don't keep years of cloud backups you never use. Less stored data means less to request.

## FAQ

**What are government data requests?** Legal demands sent to companies for user data.

**What is lawful access to data?** Access through subpoenas, court orders or warrants.

**What's the difference between a subpoena and a warrant for data?** A warrant needs a judge and probable cause and reaches more [1].

**How does the government get data from tech companies?** By sending legal process to them.

**Can I see how many requests a company gets?** Yes, in transparency reports [2][3].

## What to do next

2. Turn off location history you don't use.
3. Move sensitive chats to end-to-end encrypted apps.
