All right, guys, here's the Logan Act explained in one paragraph. It's a federal law from 1799 that bars a US citizen, acting "without authority of the United States," from dealing with a foreign government to influence it in a dispute with the US or to defeat US policy [1]. The penalty is a fine, up to three years in prison, or both [1]. In over 220 years, only two people have ever been indicted, and no one has been convicted [1].

Why does it matter here? Because when former officials meet foreign leaders, someone always asks: isn't that illegal? Here's what the law says and how it's been used.

**Quick answer**

- What is the Logan Act: a 1799 law against unauthorized private diplomacy [1].
- Logan Act prosecutions: two indictments, 1803 and 1852, both dropped [1].
- Convictions: none [1].
- Related laws for ex-officials: 18 U.S.C. § 207 and FARA [2][3].

## What does the law prohibit?

The key words: a citizen who "without authority of the United States, directly or indirectly commences or carries on any correspondence" with a foreign government [1]. The intent has to be to influence that government in a dispute with the US, or to defeat US measures [1]. The law has not been materially changed since 1799 [1].

This is the law people point to when they talk about [who supposedly served in a hidden administration](/blog/trump-shadow-cabinet-theory/).

| Element | What the law requires | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who | A US citizen | [1] |
| What | Correspondence with a foreign government | [1] |
| Without | Authority of the United States | [1] |
| Intent | Influence a dispute with the US or defeat US policy | [1] |
| Penalty | Fine, up to 3 years, or both | [1] |

## What is the history of its use?

It's named for George Logan, whose private trip to France in 1798 came before the law existed, so it never applied to him [1]. The two indictments:

- **Francis Flournoy, 1803:** a Kentucky newspaper article urging western territories to secede and ally with France [1].
- **Jacob Levy, 1852:** a letter to Mexico's president urging him not to sign a treaty with the US [1].

Both cases were dropped before trial [1]. Since then, accusations have come up again and again, with no charges:

| --- | --- | --- | --- |

| 1980s | Jesse Jackson, Jim Wright | Criticized over Cuba and Nicaragua | [1] |
| 2007 | Nancy Pelosi | Syria trip drew objections | [1] |
| 2015 | Republican senators | Letter to Iran drew objections | [1] |

## How does it apply to former officials?

The same way it applies to every citizen. A former Secretary of State is a private citizen once the term ends. Meeting a foreign leader is legal by itself; the Logan Act needs intent to defeat US policy [1].

Two other laws are more practical. Under 18 U.S.C. § 207(f), senior ex-officials can't represent or advise a foreign government to influence US decisions for one year [2]. Under FARA, anyone acting as an agent of a foreign principal in political work must register and disclose [3].

## FAQ

**What is the Logan Act?** A 1799 federal law against unauthorized dealings with foreign governments in disputes with the US [1].

**Has anyone been prosecuted under the Logan Act?** Two people were indicted, in 1803 and 1852; both cases were dropped [1].

**Has anyone been convicted?** No [1].

**Is private diplomacy illegal?** Only with the intent the Logan Act describes, and without US authority [1].
