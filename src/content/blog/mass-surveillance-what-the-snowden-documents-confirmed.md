All right, guys, a lot of people in our community were called crazy for saying the government was collecting everyone's phone records. Then, in 2013, Edward Snowden's documents showed the NSA was doing exactly that under Section 215 of the Patriot Act. That's not a theory anymore; it's in the official record. The government's own oversight board, the Privacy and Civil Liberties Oversight Board (PCLOB), reviewed the phone records program in January 2014 and recommended ending it [1]. In June 2015 Congress passed the USA FREEDOM Act, which ended the NSA's bulk collection of Americans' phone records [1].

So what did the Snowden documents reveal, what changed, and what's still unknown? Here's lawful access, workplace monitoring, what was confirmed and what remains unknown.

**Quick answer**

- What did Snowden reveal: bulk collection of US phone metadata and large-scale internet surveillance programs.
- NSA mass surveillance: confirmed for phone records under Section 215; ended by law in 2015 [1].
- Snowden documents: led to official reviews, court cases and new laws [1].
- Still debated: Section 702, which allows collection of foreigners' communications that can include Americans'.

## Lawful access

Not all government access is mass surveillance. Most is targeted: a court order or a warrant for one person's data. In 2018 the Supreme Court ruled in Carpenter v. United States that police generally need a warrant to get historical cell-phone location records [2]. Read [how authorities request data from companies](/blog/government-data-requests/).

## What was confirmed?

| Program | What it did | What happened |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Section 215 phone records | Bulk collection of call metadata | PCLOB recommended ending it; USA FREEDOM Act ended bulk collection in 2015 [1] |
| Section 702 | Collection of foreigners' communications from US companies | Still in place; repeatedly debated and extended |
| Metadata analysis | Linking people through call records | Shown to reveal sensitive details [3] |

The phone program collected metadata, not the content of calls. As we covered in our metadata guide, that can still reveal a lot: a Stanford study found phone metadata could point to medical conditions and gun ownership [3].

## What remains unknown?

This isn't a "trust me" thing. This is "verify me."

1. **The full scope of current programs.** Much is still classified.
2. **How often Americans' data is searched** under Section 702.
3. **What private companies share** beyond legal orders.

And Section 702 is a live fight: in 2026, Congress passed a series of short-term extensions while debating a longer renewal. Check its current status on Congress.gov.

## A closer look: why it matters for ordinary people

Most people will never be a target of intelligence surveillance. The bigger everyday lesson from Snowden is about data in general: once data exists, someone will want to use it. The less unnecessary data you create and store, the less there is to collect, leak or misuse.

## FAQ

**What did Snowden reveal?** Bulk collection of US phone records and large internet surveillance programs.

**Did the NSA collect everyone's phone records?** It collected bulk call metadata until the 2015 USA FREEDOM Act ended that [1].

**Is mass surveillance still happening?** Bulk phone collection ended; Section 702 collection continues and is debated.

**Do police need a warrant for location data?** Generally for historical cell-site records, per Carpenter [2].

**Does metadata matter?** Yes; it reveals a lot [3].
