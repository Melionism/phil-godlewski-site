All right, guys, the end of gold standard 1971 is the date everyone in our world talks about. Here's what happened. On the evening of August 15, 1971, President Nixon announced that foreign governments could no longer swap dollars for gold [1]. The plan was made at Camp David from August 13 to 15 with advisers including Arthur Burns, John Connally and Paul Volcker [1]. It came with a 90-day wage and price freeze and a 10% import surcharge [1]. From that point the world's money system was, in effect, fiat [1].

Here's the pressure on gold, Nixon's decision, the immediate effects and the long-term legacy.

**Quick answer**

- Nixon shock: August 15, 1971 [1].
- Why did the US leave the gold standard: too many dollars abroad for the gold it held.
- Nixon ends gold standard: closed the "gold window" for foreign governments [1].
- Legacy: the dollar is backed by law and trust, not metal.

## The pressure on gold

Under Bretton Woods, the US promised gold at $35 an ounce [2]. As dollars piled up abroad, other countries could ask for more gold than the US had. See [how currency evolved over time](/blog/history-money/).

## Nixon's decision

| Step | What it did | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gold window closed | No more dollars-for-gold for governments | [1] |
| Wage and price freeze | 90 days, first outside wartime | [1] |
| Import surcharge | 10% on imports | [1] |
| Smithsonian agreement | Tried to keep pegged rates; failed soon after | [1] |

## Immediate effects

Markets had to adjust. The Smithsonian agreement tried to save fixed rates a few months later, but the Bretton Woods system ended soon after [1].

## Long-term legacy

I get it, I get it. A lot of people see 1971 as the moment money lost its anchor. Others see it as the moment the Fed got room to fight recessions. Both views are part of the real debate. What's not debatable: since 1971, the dollar has stayed the world's main currency, at about 58% of disclosed reserves in 2024 [3]. Read [the postwar deal](/blog/bretton-woods-system/).
